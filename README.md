## What size dog crate does your dog actually need?

Your dog should be able to stand up, turn around, and lie down fully stretched — and the crate should be no bigger than that. Measure nose to tail base and floor to ear tips, then add a few inches. That's the whole rule.

Two common mistakes work in opposite directions. A crate that's too small is uncomfortable and can make crate training harder. But a crate that's much too big loses its den-like appeal, and a dog being house-trained will treat one corner as a bathroom. For a puppy, buy the adult size and use a divider panel to shrink the living space while the dog grows — most quality wire crates include one, and it's the single most useful "extra" to look for.

As a reference point, the MidWest iCrate's 42-inch model measures 42.5 x 28.5 x 30.5 inches and is rated for dogs between 71 and 90 pounds — Australian Shepherds, Boxers, and Golden Retrievers territory. If your dog sits near the top of a size range, sizing up one model is the safer call.

## Which type of crate fits your situation?

Wire crates are the default for a reason. They fold flat for storage and travel, they're ventilated on all sides, and entry-level models start around $26. The tradeoff is security: a wire crate handles a normal dog fine, but it's not built for a dog that tests latches. The better wire models address this with slide-bolt latches plus a locking guard — MidWest calls theirs Paw Block — and rounded corner clips to prevent snags.

Heavy-duty crates are the answer for escape artists. These use steel tube frames with multiple locking points; the Feandrea 42-inch model, for example, uses five L-shaped locks, has a small feeding door in addition to the main door, and converts into a playpen by removing the top. Expect to pay several times the price of a wire crate, and expect it to be heavy. Specialty brands like Impact Dog Crates and Diggs occupy the high end — Diggs' crate lineup runs roughly $239 to $1,149 depending on size and model. Most owners never need to spend that.

Soft-sided crates are for travel and calm dogs only. They're light and fold up small, but one motivated dog turns the fabric into a door. Fine for a car ride with a settled adult dog; wrong tool for crate training or for chewers.

Air travel is its own rulebook, and it's stricter than most people expect:

- Welded wire mesh crates are barred from air transport.
- Two-piece plastic shells must be bolted together with metal hardware — clips alone don't qualify.
- Ventilation must cover all four sides.
- Wheels must be removed.
- The water bowl has to be refillable from outside the crate.
- "Live Animals" labels are mandatory.

If a flight is in your dog's future, check the crate against that list before buying anything else. The Petmate Sky Kennel is the model that regularly appears in expert roundups for this exact purpose.
